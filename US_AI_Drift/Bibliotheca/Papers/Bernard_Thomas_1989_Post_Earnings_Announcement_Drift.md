# A zero-cost portfolio long the most positive earnings surprises and short the most negative ones earned about 18% annualized in the quarters after the announcement, and the drift is not explained by risk

Bernard and Thomas tested whether the well-known tendency of a stock to keep drifting in the
direction of an earnings surprise, after the announcement, is compensation for risk (as market
efficiency would require) or a genuine delayed response. They found it is the latter: investors
fail to fully recognize what a current earnings surprise implies about future earnings, so the
price keeps correcting over the following months instead of adjusting once, on the announcement
day. This is the foundational citation for the anomaly this whole hypothesis is named after —
post-earnings-announcement drift, PEAD.

> This paper is the base rate this hypothesis has to beat, not confirm. PEAD, on its own, in a
> broad market, is real but modest and has been known for over three decades — it is close to a
> textbook anomaly by now, which means any edge a generic PEAD screen still finds today is likely
> small and crowded. The claim here is narrower and has to earn its place against this base rate:
> that the drift is **unusually strong inside a hindsight-labelled "AI-exposed" universe**,
> specifically because a beat driven by a new, hard-to-model revenue line (AI-related sales) is
> under-trusted for longer than a beat in a mature, well-understood line of business. If the
> measured drift in `US_AI_Drift/Universe/` does not clear this paper's original ~18% annualized
> figure by a meaningful margin, the "AI-specific" part of the claim has not been shown — a result
> in line with generic PEAD would say only that the well-known anomaly still exists, not that this
> universe is special.
